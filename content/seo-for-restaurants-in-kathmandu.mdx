## How Kathmandu diners actually choose a restaurant

Picture the real journey. A tourist lands in Kathmandu and searches "best restaurant in Thamel" before leaving the hotel. A local in Lazimpat types "momo near me" at lunch. A couple planning a birthday searches "rooftop restaurant Kathmandu" a week ahead. A group of friends argues about dinner and someone searches "thakali set near Durbarmarg" on the spot.

**Every one of those searches is a table you can win or lose.** The restaurants that win are the ones with a complete Google Business Profile, recent reviews, real photos of the food, and a website that answers the exact question being asked — not a Facebook page last updated in 2022.

This is the biggest difference between restaurant SEO and general business SEO: your customer is usually hungry, nearby, and deciding within minutes. Speed matters more, photos matter more, and reviews matter more than anywhere else. A restaurant with 300 recent reviews and fresh photos will beat a restaurant with a prettier website and 12 reviews from 2021.

## The three searches that matter most

### 1. "Best [dish] in [area]"

These are high-intent searches. Someone typing "best momo in Thamel" or "best thakali in Kathmandu" is close to eating. To rank here you need a page that actually talks about that dish: what makes yours different, real photos, prices, and honest detail. A menu page that lists "Momo — Rs. 250" with no description ranks for nothing and convinces nobody.

### 2. "Near me" and map searches

On a phone, "restaurant near me" and map results dominate. This is local SEO, and it runs on your Google Business Profile: correct name, address and phone number, accurate opening hours, your real menu, and a steady flow of reviews. My guide to [local SEO for Kathmandu businesses](/blog/local-seo-kathmandu-google-business-profile) walks through the full setup — for restaurants, the profile is not a nice-to-have, it is the storefront.

### 3. Occasion and experience searches

"Rooftop restaurant Kathmandu", "birthday dinner Kathmandu", "vegan restaurant Patan", "family restaurant with parking" — these searches are how restaurants get found by people who have not chosen a place yet. The restaurants that rank are the ones with a page or a post that answers the occasion: photos of the rooftop at sunset, a set menu for groups, a clear note about parking or a kids' menu. One honest, specific page beats a homepage that tries to answer everything.

## Your Google Business Profile is your real homepage

For most Kathmandu restaurants, the Google Business Profile gets seen far more than the website. Diners see it in search results and on Maps before they ever click through. Treat it like your front door:

- **Complete every field.** Category (not just "Restaurant" — add "Momo restaurant", "Cafe", "Rooftop restaurant" where they fit), opening hours including holidays, phone number, and a link to your menu.
- **Upload real photos every week.** Your actual dishes, your actual dining room, your actual view. Stock photos of food you do not serve will get you bad reviews from disappointed diners.
- **Post weekly.** A new dish, a festival special, a lunch set — Google Business posts are free, and they keep your profile looking alive.
- **Answer every review, good and bad.** 97% of review readers also read the business's responses. A calm, human reply to a bad review convinces the next reader more than the complaint itself.
- **Keep hours accurate.** Nothing loses a table faster than a diner arriving to a closed restaurant Google said was open. Update hours for Dashain, Tihar and public holidays.

## The menu is an SEO asset — treat it like one

Here is a mistake almost every Kathmandu restaurant makes: the menu exists only as a photo on Facebook, or as a PDF that Google cannot read. If Google cannot read your menu, you cannot rank for the dishes people search for.

- **Put the menu on your website as real text**, not just an image or a PDF. Every dish name, description and price should be text Google can index.
- **Use the words diners actually search.** "Buff momo", "chicken thakali set", "Newari khaja set", "vegan thali" — these are real searches. Your menu page should use them naturally.
- **One page per signature offering.** A page for your momo, a page for your thakali set, a page for your rooftop dinner experience. Each targets one search and answers it completely. This is the same principle behind good [keyword research for the Nepal market](/blog/keyword-research-nepal-english-vs-nepali-search): match one page to one real search.
- **Keep prices current.** Outdated prices on Google are the fastest way to earn a one-star review from someone who came for the Rs. 300 lunch set and found it is now Rs. 450.

## Reviews: the ranking factor you can actually control

Reviews do three jobs at once for a restaurant: they convince diners, they feed Google's local ranking, and they tell you what to fix. A Harvard Business School study found that a one-star increase in rating can mean 5–9% more revenue for a restaurant. That is the size of the prize.

- **Ask at the right moment.** The best time to ask for a review is when the diner is smiling — after the meal, at the bill, or in a short message the next day. A small card on the table with a QR code to your Google review page works quietly all day.
- **Never buy reviews or offer discounts for five stars.** Google removes fake reviews, and diners can smell them. A 4.6 with 400 honest reviews beats a suspicious 5.0 with 30.
- **Respond to every review within a day.** Thank the happy ones by name when you can. For the unhappy ones, apologise, say what you will fix, and invite them back. Future diners read your replies as carefully as the reviews.
- **Mine reviews for content.** If ten reviews praise your jhol momo, that is your headline dish — give it a page. If three reviews mention slow service at lunch, fix the service, then say so in a reply.

## SEO vs delivery apps vs social media: an honest comparison

| Channel | Cost | Speed | Control | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **SEO (Google + Maps)** | Time + effort, no per-order fee | 3–6 months to show results | Full — your profile, your pages, your customer data | Long-term walk-ins and direct orders |
| **Food-delivery apps** | Commission on every order | Instant visibility | Low — their rules, their customer relationship | Filling slow hours, reaching app-only diners |
| **Facebook / Instagram ads** | Pay per click or per impression | Days | Medium — you control the ad, not the cost | A new opening, a festival offer, a specific promotion |
| **Instagram / TikTok content** | Time, sometimes paid promotion | Weeks to build an audience | Medium — the algorithm decides reach | Discovery and cravings, especially for photogenic dishes |

**The honest answer is that most successful Kathmandu restaurants use more than one.** Delivery apps fill slow hours today. Ads push a specific offer this week. SEO is the channel that slowly replaces both — because a diner who finds you on Google and walks in costs you nothing in commission, and they are your customer, not the app's.

The mistake is treating delivery apps as the whole strategy. Every order sold through an app pays commission forever. Every walk-in your Google profile earns is a full-margin table, this month and next year.

## What actually ranks a restaurant website

### A fast website that works on a phone

Your diner is on a phone, often on slow mobile data, deciding in seconds. If your site takes more than a few seconds to load, they leave — and Google notices. Compress your food photos (a restaurant site lives or dies on photos, but a 5 MB hero image kills your speed), keep the phone number and directions visible without scrolling, and test the site on a real phone, not just a laptop. My guide to [Core Web Vitals and site speed for Nepal's mobile users](/blog/core-web-vitals-site-speed-nepal-mobile) explains what to measure and how to fix it.

### Real photos, honest prices, clear information

Diners book with their eyes and their wallets. Use real photos of your actual dishes — not stock photos of food you do not serve. Show prices or at least a clear price range. State what matters: vegetarian and vegan options, seating, parking, wifi, whether you take reservations, and how to find you. Vague sites lose to specific sites, because a diner comparing five tabs will choose the one that answered their questions.

### Location pages for each neighbourhood you serve

If you have one restaurant in Thamel and one in Pokhara, they need separate pages — each with its own address, hours, menu highlights and reviews. Even a single restaurant benefits from a page that names its neighbourhood properly: "rooftop restaurant in Jhamsikhel" is a real search, and a page that says so, with directions from a known landmark, has a genuine chance to rank for it.

### Content that answers diner questions

A short blog post or guide page can rank for searches your menu never will: "where to eat in Thamel", "best breakfast in Kathmandu", "what is a Newari khaja set". This is the same idea behind my guide to [content that ranks for Nepali businesses](/blog/content-that-ranks-blogging-strategy-nepal): one useful page per real question, written for the diner, not for Google.

## Honest tradeoffs

- **SEO is slow.** Expect 3–6 months before local rankings move in a meaningful way, and longer for competitive searches like "best restaurant in Kathmandu". If you need tables full this weekend, run a promotion — then start SEO for next quarter. My guide to [how long SEO takes for Nepali businesses](/blog/how-long-does-seo-take-nepal) sets realistic timelines.
- **You cannot outsource the reviews.** An SEO freelancer can fix your website, but only your staff can earn reviews — one happy diner at a time. If service is inconsistent, SEO will bring more people to read about it.
- **The map pack is competitive.** Thamel alone has hundreds of restaurants fighting for three map spots. Winning takes a complete profile, more reviews than your neighbours, and a website that backs it up. There is no shortcut, and anyone selling one is selling spam.
- **Bad food cannot be SEO'd.** Ranking first with a 3.2-star rating fills tables once — then the reviews do the rest. Fix the product first, then amplify it.

## FAQs

### How long does SEO take for a restaurant in Kathmandu?

For local searches, expect the first visible movement in 6–12 weeks if your profile and website were weak to start with, and 3–6 months for competitive searches like "best restaurant in Thamel". Restaurants have one advantage over other businesses: Google Business Profile changes can show up in days, and a burst of genuine reviews can move map rankings faster than website changes alone.

### Do I need a website, or is a Google Business Profile enough?

A Google Business Profile alone can get you into the map results, and for a small eatery that may be enough to start. But a website is what lets you rank for dish and occasion searches, publish your menu as readable text, and take reservations directly. Restaurants with a fast, complete website consistently outrank profile-only competitors for everything beyond "near me".

### How many reviews do I need to rank on Google Maps?

There is no fixed number, but you need more — and more recent — reviews than the restaurants you are competing with for the same searches. In busy areas like Thamel, the top map results often have hundreds of reviews. What matters most is a steady flow: ten new reviews a month beats fifty reviews from two years ago.

### Should I pay for ads instead of SEO?

Ads work fast and SEO works long. If you are launching, promoting a festival menu, or filling tables this week, ads are the right tool. But ads stop the moment you stop paying, while a top map ranking keeps bringing diners every day. Most restaurants should do both: ads for the push, SEO for the base. My comparison of [SEO vs Facebook and Instagram ads in Nepal](/blog/seo-vs-facebook-ads-nepal) breaks down when each makes sense.

### What is the most common SEO mistake Kathmandu restaurants make?

Putting the menu only on Facebook or as a PDF. Google cannot read either properly, so the restaurant cannot rank for its own dishes. The second most common mistake is an incomplete Google Business Profile — wrong hours, no menu link, no photos — which costs tables every single day.

### How much does restaurant SEO cost in Nepal?

It depends on whether you do it yourself or hire help, and on how competitive your area is. The work itself — profile setup, menu pages, site speed, review habit — is mostly time, not money. If you hire an SEO specialist, ask exactly what you get and what you should check before paying; my guide to [SEO services in Nepal](/blog/seo-services-in-nepal-what-you-get) explains what to ask before you sign anything.

### Can SEO help a restaurant outside Kathmandu?

Yes. The same principles apply in Pokhara, Chitwan, Bhaktapur or anywhere tourists and locals search on Google. In smaller towns the competition is often weaker, so a complete profile and a decent website can win the map results faster than in Thamel.

### Does Instagram matter for restaurant SEO?

Instagram does not directly rank your website, but it drives discovery — and discovery drives branded searches and reviews, which do help. A diner who finds your momo on Instagram, searches your name on Google, and leaves a review has helped your SEO. Treat social media as the top of the funnel and Google as the place the decision gets made.
