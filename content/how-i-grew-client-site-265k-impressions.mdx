A Nepali ecommerce site came to me with almost no search visibility. Eight months later, Google Search Console showed **265,000 impressions and 6,760 clicks, at an average position of 8.2**. That is the whole case study in one sentence — and the rest of this post is the honest version of how it happened, what I would do again, and what I would not promise you.

This is a real result from my own work, not a composite and not a screenshot borrowed from the internet. It is for business owners and marketers in Nepal who want to know what SEO actually looks like when it works — and what it costs in time and patience. It is **not** a guarantee. Your site, your market, and your starting point are different, and anyone who promises you these exact numbers is selling you something.

## TL;DR

- **Starting point:** a Nepali ecommerce site with near-zero Google Search visibility.
- **Result after 8 months:** 265K impressions, 6.76K clicks, average position 8.2 in Google Search Console.
- **What worked:** fixing the technical basics first, then product and category pages people actually search for, then steady content — measured every week, not guessed.
- **What did not matter:** chasing tricks, buying links, or obsessing over a single keyword.
- **The honest lesson:** SEO worked here because the site was fixable, the market was winnable, and the work was consistent for eight months. Remove any one of those three and the result changes.

A few things worth saying about these numbers, because numbers without context mislead:

- **Impressions are not sales.** 265K impressions means the site appeared in search results 265,000 times. 6.76K clicks means people actually visited. What those visitors bought is the client's business, not a number I publish.
- **Average position 8.2 is page one — barely.** It means the site's pages were, on average, showing near the bottom of page one. That is a real achievement from near-zero, and also a long way from dominating the results. There was still plenty of headroom.
- **The growth was not a straight line.** Early months moved slowly. The visible jump came later, once the fixes and content had time to compound. That pattern is normal — see my guide on [how long SEO takes for Nepali businesses](/blog/how-long-does-seo-take-nepal).

## The Starting Point

The site was a working ecommerce store with real products and real customers — it was not a broken or abandoned website. What it lacked was search visibility: Google barely knew it existed, and the pages that did show up ranked too far down to matter.

That is a common situation for Nepali businesses. The website exists, the business is real, but nobody built it to be found. The owner had not done anything wrong — they had simply never had anyone treat search as a channel worth investing in. ## What I Actually Did

There was no single trick. The work fell into four phases, in this order — and the order mattered.

### Phase 1: Fix the technical foundation

Before writing a word of new content, I audited the site the way I describe in my [technical SEO audit checklist for Nepali websites](/blog/technical-seo-audit-checklist-nepal): crawling, indexing, speed, mobile usability, and the basics Google needs to understand a site at all. Problems that stop Google from crawling or understanding your pages make everything else pointless — content on a site Google cannot read does not rank.

This phase is unglamorous and invisible to the client. Nothing about the site looks different to a visitor. But it is the phase that makes the rest of the work possible, and skipping it is the most common reason SEO engagements fail quietly.

### Phase 2: Fix the pages that should already rank

Next came the product and category pages — the pages closest to a purchase. Most of them had thin descriptions, missing or duplicated titles, and no clear answer to what the customer was searching for. I rewrote them around the actual search terms Nepali customers use, in the language they use, following the [on-page SEO basics every Nepali business should get right](/blog/on-page-seo-basics-nepali-businesses).

For an ecommerce site, this is where the money is. A product page that ranks for what people actually type into Google brings visitors who are ready to buy — not browsers, buyers. My guide to [ecommerce SEO in Nepal](/blog/ecommerce-seo-in-nepal) goes deeper on product pages, categories, and site speed.

### Phase 3: Publish content that answers real questions

Once the money pages were fixed, I added content aimed at the questions customers ask before they buy: how to choose, what to compare, what something costs, whether it is worth it. Not blog posts for the sake of blogging — pages built around real search demand, written to answer the question properly.

This is the slowest phase to show results and the easiest to cut when budgets tighten. It is also the phase that built most of the impressions: useful pages earn rankings for hundreds of related searches, not just one target keyword. My guide on [content that ranks](/blog/content-that-ranks-blogging-strategy-nepal) explains the strategy behind it.

### Phase 4: Measure, and keep fixing what the data showed

Every week, Google Search Console told me what was working: which pages were gaining impressions, which queries were just below page one, which pages got clicks and which got ignored. The work after month three was mostly responding to that data — improving pages that were close to ranking, fixing pages that got impressions but no clicks, and dropping ideas the data did not support.

This is the part of SEO that separates a process from a one-off project. Anyone can fix a site once. Ranking compounds because someone keeps watching the numbers and keeps adjusting. If you hire an SEO, ask them what they measure and how often — my guide to [SEO services in Nepal](/blog/seo-services-in-nepal-what-you-get) lists the questions worth asking before you pay.

## What I Would Do Again

- **Technical first, content second.** Content on a broken foundation wastes the effort.
- **Product and category pages before blog posts.** The pages closest to revenue deserve the work first.
- **One person owning both the code and the SEO.** I am a developer as well as an SEO, so a technical fix did not wait for a separate developer, a ticket, and a sprint. That speed matters — a fix that takes three weeks to ship is a fix that does not compound.
- **Weekly measurement in Search Console.** Small, regular decisions beat big quarterly guesses.

## What Did Not Matter (or Would Have Hurt)

- **No bought links, no link schemes, no spam.** The site earned its rankings from being a better answer, not from manufactured authority. Spammy links are the fastest way to trade a short-term bump for a long-term problem.
- **No obsession with a single keyword.** The 265K impressions came from many pages ranking for many searches, not one trophy keyword. Chasing one keyword would have narrowed the whole effort.
- **No redesign.** The site did not need to look different. It needed to be understood by Google and useful to visitors. A redesign would have burned the budget and risked breaking what already worked.

## The Honest Tradeoffs

- **Eight months is a long time.** The client paid for months of work before the numbers looked impressive. If your business cannot fund that runway, SEO may be the wrong first investment — ads or direct outreach may serve you better right now.
- **These results are not typical for every site.** This site had real products, a working store, and a market where the competition was beatable. A site in a saturated niche, or one with no clear offer, would not have produced these numbers in eight months.
- **Growth slowed the need for new work, it did not end it.** Rankings need maintenance: competitors improve, Google updates, pages go stale. The 8.2 average position was a checkpoint, not a finish line.
- **I cannot show you the client's name, niche, or revenue.** That limits how much you can verify independently, and I would rather say that plainly than dress the case study up with details I cannot share.

## FAQs

### Is 265K impressions in 8 months a good result?

For a Nepali ecommerce site starting from near-zero, yes — it is a strong result. But impressions alone are not the goal; the 6.76K clicks and the 8.2 average position are what make it meaningful. A site can have huge impressions and almost no clicks if it ranks for the wrong searches. Judge any SEO result by clicks and position, not impressions alone.

### Could my business get the same result?

Maybe, and maybe not. It depends on your starting point, your market, and how competitive your keywords are. A business in a thin Nepal market with a fixable site can move fast. A business competing against large, established sites for the same keywords will take longer. Any honest SEO will audit your site before promising a timeline.

### How much did it cost?

I do not publish client fees, and you should be suspicious of any case study that ties a result to a price without context. What I can say is that the work was steady and monthly, not a one-off project — and that the client kept going because the numbers kept improving, not because of a contract.

### Why did it take 8 months and not 3?

Because Google needs time to crawl changes, re-evaluate pages, and trust a site it previously ignored. The first months were mostly foundation work with little visible movement. The visible growth came in the second half, once the foundation, the fixed pages, and the new content were all working together. SEO compounds; it does not switch on.

### What was the single biggest factor?

If I had to pick one, it would be fixing the pages that were closest to a purchase — product and category pages — before anything else. Those pages had the clearest search demand and the clearest path to revenue. Content and technical fixes mattered, but the money pages were the lever.

### Did you use AI to write the content?

The content was written to answer real customer questions properly, in natural language. Whether a draft starts with a human or a tool matters less than whether the final page is genuinely useful — Google rewards pages that satisfy the searcher, and visitors can tell the difference. Thin, generic content does not rank, however it was produced.

### What would you do differently now?

I would start the measurement habit even earlier — tracking which queries were close to page one from the first month, so the improvement work could start sooner. And I would set expectations even more clearly at the start: the slow first months are the hardest part of any SEO engagement, and clients who understand that stay the course.

### How do I check if an SEO's case study is real?

Ask for the Search Console data, not a screenshot of a ranking tool. Ask what the starting point was, what the timeframe was, and what the site sells. A real case study has a starting point, a method, and numbers that can be checked. If someone cannot explain how they got a result, they probably cannot repeat it for you.
