A tourist in Thamel types "best hotel in Kathmandu" into Google. A Nepali family in Pokhara types "पोखरामा राम्रो होटल" — the same question, in Devanagari script. And a college student in Butwal types "sasto hotel Kathmandu" — Nepali words, written in Roman letters. Three searches, one intent, three completely different keywords. If your website only targets the English version, you are invisible to two out of three of these customers.

That is the core problem of keyword research in Nepal: **your market searches in three languages at once**. English, Devanagari Nepali, and Romanized Nepali (Nepali words typed in the Latin alphabet) all show up in real search data. Most Nepali businesses only optimize for English, and they wonder why their rankings never turn into calls.

This guide is for Nepali business owners and marketers who want to find the words their customers actually type — and rank for them. It is **not** for people looking for a magic keyword tool, and it is not a replacement for a full content plan — but it covers the research step that every other SEO decision depends on.

## TL;DR

- Nepali customers search in **three forms**: English ("hotel in Kathmandu"), Devanagari Nepali ("काठमाडौं होटल"), and Romanized Nepali ("sasto hotel Kathmandu"). All three are real searches with real customers behind them.
- **English dominates high-value commercial searches** — hotels, flights, gadgets, services for tourists and professionals. **Nepali dominates everyday, local, and how-to searches** — food, festivals, government services, local shops.
- Start free with **Google Keyword Planner, Google Search Console, and Google's own autocomplete and "People also ask"** before paying for any tool.
- Target **one search intent per page**, not one keyword per page. A page about trekking permits can rank for the English, Nepali, and Romanized versions of the same question.
- **Do not translate your English keywords word-for-word into Nepali.** People search the way they speak, and the way they speak rarely matches a dictionary translation.
- Measure what matters: **clicks, calls, and enquiries from search** — not how many keywords you rank for.

## Why Nepal's Search Market Is Different

In most countries, keyword research is a one-language job. In Nepal, it is three jobs at once, because the same person switches languages depending on what they are looking for.

**English is the language of buying.** When money is involved — booking a hotel, hiring a service, buying a phone — many Nepali searchers switch to English, even if Nepali is their first language. English keywords also capture tourists and the Nepali diaspora searching from abroad. If you sell something expensive or serve visitors, English keywords are where the revenue sits.

**Devanagari Nepali is the language of everyday life.** Recipes, festival dates, local news, government procedures, and neighbourhood services are searched in Nepali script far more often than in English. A clinic in Lalitpur will get more searches for "ललितपुरमा दाँतको डाक्टर" than for "dentist in Lalitpur" — because the person searching is a local resident, not a tourist.

**Romanized Nepali is the language of the phone keyboard.** Typing Devanagari on a phone takes effort; switching to an English keyboard is one tap. So millions of Nepali searches are Nepali words typed in Roman letters: "sasto mobile", "kathmandu ma ramro restaurant", "nepal ma job". These searches are real, they have volume, and almost nobody optimizes for them — which makes them some of the easiest wins in Nepali SEO.

**The same intent, three keywords.** "Best trekking company in Nepal", "नेपालमा राम्रो ट्रेकिङ कम्पनी", and "ramro trekking company Nepal" are the same customer with the same wallet. A business that ranks for all three gets three times the chances of being found. A business that ranks for one gets a third of its market.

## Step 1: Start With Your Customers, Not a Tool

Every good keyword list starts away from the keyboard. Before opening any tool, write down the answers to three questions:

1. **What do your customers call what you sell?** Not what you call it — what they call it. A "dental clinic" is a "दाँतको अस्पताल" to many patients. A "boutique hotel" is just a "hotel" to a traveller. Use their words.
2. **What problem are they trying to solve?** People rarely search for a service name. They search for the problem: "tooth pain treatment Kathmandu", "wedding venue Pokhara price", "how to renew driving license Nepal".
3. **Where are they?** A searcher in Kathmandu, Pokhara, Chitwan, or Butwal types the city name differently — and Google's results change by location. Your keywords should carry your city, not just your service.

Write down 20–30 seed phrases from these answers, in all three forms. If you run a restaurant in Thamel, your list might include "best restaurant in Thamel", "थामेलमा राम्रो रेस्टुरेन्ट", "thamel ma ramro restaurant", "momo in Thamel", "rooftop restaurant Kathmandu". That raw list is the foundation of everything that follows.

## Step 2: Expand the List Free — With Google Itself

You do not need a paid tool to do keyword research in Nepal. Google gives you the data directly, and its data is the most accurate there is, because it comes from real searches.

**Google autocomplete.** Type a seed phrase into Google and watch the suggestions. These are real searches people have made. Type "hotel in Kathmandu" and you will see "hotel in Kathmandu price", "hotel in Kathmandu for couple", "hotel in Kathmandu near airport" — each one a keyword worth targeting. Do the same in Devanagari and in Romanized Nepali. Autocomplete works in all three.

**"People also ask" and related searches.** Scroll down any Google results page and you will find the questions people actually ask, and the searches related to yours. These are your FAQ topics and your supporting keywords, handed to you free.

**Google Search Console.** If your site is already live, Search Console shows the exact queries that already bring you impressions — including the Nepali and Romanized queries you never thought to target. A page ranking at position 18 for a Nepali query you did not know existed is a page one small push away from real traffic. Check this before anything else: it tells you what Google already thinks your site is relevant for.

**Google Keyword Planner.** Free inside a Google Ads account (you never have to run an ad). It gives rough search volumes and, more usefully, new keyword ideas grouped by theme. Take its volume numbers as rough guides, not gospel — volumes for Nepali-language keywords are often underreported, because fewer advertisers bid on them. A keyword Planner says has "low volume" can still bring real customers if it is exactly what a buyer types.

**Your own website's search box, your inbox, and your phone.** What do customers ask you on WhatsApp and Facebook Messenger? What do they type into your site's search box? What do they ask on the phone? Those exact phrases are keywords. A question you hear three times a week is a keyword worth a page.

## Step 3: Judge Every Keyword by Intent, Not Volume

The biggest mistake in keyword research is chasing the biggest number. A keyword with huge search volume and the wrong intent brings visitors who never buy. A keyword with modest volume and buying intent brings customers.

Sort every keyword into one of four intents:

| Intent | What the searcher wants | Example | Value to you |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Informational** | To learn something | "how to renew passport Nepal", "dashain 2026 date" | Builds trust and traffic, rarely sells today |
| **Navigational** | To find a specific business | "Griffity Studios", "your business name" | They already know you — be findable |
| **Commercial** | To compare before buying | "best hotel in Pokhara price", "top SEO expert Nepal" | Close to buying — this is your money keyword |
| **Transactional** | To buy or book now | "book hotel Kathmandu", "dentist appointment Lalitpur" | Highest value, lowest volume, hardest to win |

For most Nepali businesses, **commercial and transactional keywords pay the bills**. Informational keywords build the audience that later searches commercially. You need both — but if you only have time for one, start with the keywords people type when their wallet is already open.

A practical test for any keyword: **if someone searched this and landed on your page, would they find exactly what they were looking for — and could they become a customer?** If the answer is no, the keyword is wrong for that page, no matter how popular it is.

## Step 4: Don't Translate — Transcribe How People Actually Search

This is where most Nepali keyword research goes wrong. A business takes its English keyword list, runs it through translation, and targets the Devanagari version. The result looks correct and ranks for nothing, because nobody searches that way.

**People search the way they speak, not the way a dictionary writes.** The formal Nepali for "cheap hotel" is "सस्तो होटल" — but many searchers type "sasto hotel", or "cheap hotel Kathmandu", or "budget hotel Kathmandu". The formal Nepali for "how to apply for a passport" is long and correct — but people type short, spoken versions. Your job is to find the spoken version, and autocomplete and Search Console are how you find it.

**Mixing is normal.** Nepali searches are full of English words dropped into Nepali sentences: "Kathmandu ma best gym", "nepal ma iphone price", "Pokhara hotel booking". These mixed queries are often the highest-volume version of a keyword, because they match exactly how people think. Target them as they are — do not "correct" them into pure English or pure Nepali.

**Spelling varies, and that is an opportunity.** Romanized Nepali has no standard spelling: "sasto", "sastoo", "shasto" can all appear. Google is good at matching variants, so you do not need a page for every spelling — but your page should use the most common one in its headings and text, and mention the variants naturally where it makes sense.

**Test with real searches, not with your own judgement.** Before committing to a Nepali keyword, search it yourself and look at the results. Do the top pages look like businesses like yours? Do they answer the same question your page answers? If the results are news articles, government pages, or completely different businesses, Google thinks that keyword means something else — and your page will not rank for it, however good it is.

## Step 5: Map One Intent to One Page

Once you have a keyword list, the next mistake is putting all of them on one page. Google ranks pages, not websites, and a page that tries to rank for everything ranks for nothing.

The rule: **one search intent, one page.** Group your keywords by the question they answer:

- "Best hotel in Kathmandu", "काठमाडौंमा राम्रो होटल", and "ramro hotel Kathmandu" are one intent — one page, which can naturally use all three forms in its headings, body, and FAQs.
- "Hotel in Kathmandu price" and "Kathmandu hotel booking" are different intents — price comparison and booking — and deserve their own pages or sections.
- "How to get to Kathmandu from Pokhara" is informational — a guide, not a sales page.

Within a page, use the English version in the main heading if that is where the money is, and weave the Nepali and Romanized versions into subheadings, FAQs, and body text where they read naturally. A FAQ section is the easiest place to do this honestly: a question asked in Romanized Nepali, answered in plain language, is genuinely useful content — and it is exactly the format Google's AI search features love to quote.

**Long-tail keywords are your best friend in Nepal.** "Best restaurant in Kathmandu" is fought over by every restaurant in the city. "Best Newari restaurant in Bhaktapur for family dinner" has fewer searches — but every one of them is a customer ready to book, and almost nobody is competing for it. In a small market like Nepal, long-tail keywords are not a consolation prize. They are the strategy.

## What About Keyword Difficulty and Paid Tools?

Sooner or later someone will try to sell you a keyword tool. Here is the honest position: **paid tools help, but they are not required, and they are weaker in Nepal than in big English-speaking markets.** Their Nepali-language data is thinner, their volumes are estimates, and their difficulty scores are built for markets with far more competition than Nepal has.

If you do use one — Ahrefs, Semrush, or a similar tool — use it for two things: finding keyword ideas you missed, and checking how hard a keyword really is by looking at the pages already ranking for it. If the top ten results are weak pages — thin content, slow sites, no real business behind them — you can outrank them with a genuinely useful page, whatever the tool's difficulty score says. If the top ten are strong, established sites, pick a longer, more specific version of the keyword instead.

What paid tools cannot do is tell you what your customers call things. Only your customers can tell you that. The tool refines the list; it never creates it.

## Honest Tradeoffs

- **Targeting three language forms takes more work.** A page that serves English, Devanagari, and Romanized searchers needs more thought than a page in one language. The payoff is a market your competitors are ignoring — most Nepali businesses only do English — so the extra effort is where the advantage lives.
- **Nepali keywords often show lower volume in tools.** That is partly real and partly a measurement gap — tools undercount Nepali-language searches. Judge a Nepali keyword by the customers it brings, not by a tool's estimate. A keyword that brings five bookings a month beats a keyword that brings five hundred visitors who never call.
- **Ranking in Nepali takes the same time as ranking in English.** There is no shortcut: new pages still need weeks to months to climb, and competitive English keywords still need authority. Nepali and Romanized keywords are often *easier* because fewer businesses compete for them — but easy does not mean instant. See my guide on [how long SEO takes in Nepal](/blog/how-long-does-seo-take-nepal) for realistic timelines.
- **Translation is not localization.** A page translated word-for-word from English into Nepali usually reads stiffly and misses the phrases people actually search. If Nepali content matters to your business, have it written by someone who searches in Nepali — or at least reviewed by one.

## FAQs

### Should my website be in English or Nepali?

Both, where it matters. English for your main commercial pages — services, pricing, booking — because that is where the buying searches are. Nepali (Devanagari and Romanized) in your blog, FAQs, and local guides, because that is where everyday and how-to searches are. A hotel does not need its booking page in Nepali, but a blog post answering "पोखरामा घुम्न जाने ठाउँ" can bring the travellers who later book the English page.

### How do I find Nepali keywords if tools show no volume?

Use Google itself: autocomplete, "People also ask", related searches, and your Search Console data. Tools underreport Nepali keywords because fewer advertisers bid on them, so "no volume" in a tool often means "no advertiser data", not "no searches". If autocomplete suggests it and Search Console shows impressions for it, people are searching it.

### What is Romanized Nepali, and should I really target it?

Romanized Nepali is Nepali written in the Latin alphabet — "sasto hotel Kathmandu" instead of "सस्तो होटल काठमाडौं". People type it because switching to a Devanagari keyboard on a phone is slow. Yes, target it: it is one of the least competitive, highest-intent keyword spaces in Nepal, because almost no business optimizes for it deliberately.

### How many keywords should one page target?

One primary keyword — the main phrase the page is about — plus a handful of closely related variations that share the same intent, including the Nepali and Romanized forms of the same question. If a keyword needs a different answer, it needs a different page. Ten loosely related keywords on one page is keyword stuffing with extra steps.

### Is it okay to mix English and Nepali on the same page?

Yes — it is how Nepali customers actually search and read. A page that says "Best time to visit Pokhara" and answers a FAQ in Romanized Nepali is not messy; it is realistic. Keep the mix natural: English headings for the main topic, Nepali and Romanized forms where a real customer would use them, and never force a language into a sentence where it does not belong.

### How do I know if my keyword research is working?

Watch three things in Search Console and your analytics: **impressions** for your target keywords going up, **average position** improving for the pages you optimized, and — most importantly — **enquiries, calls, and bookings from organic search** going up. Rankings without enquiries mean you picked the wrong keywords. Enquiries going up means you picked the right ones.
